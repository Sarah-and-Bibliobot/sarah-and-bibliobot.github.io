In preparation for starting my masters this year, I reduced my hours at work. It has been so long since I have been in formal education, that I wanted to give myself the best chance of succeeding.

One of my plans for my 'spare' days before starting, has been to take some online self-paced courses, to get myself into 'study mode', and one relevant course I did was [Japanese Books: From Manuscripts to Print](https://www.edx.org/learn/japanese/harvard-university-japanese-books-from-manuscript-to-print), run by HarvardX.

<!--more-->

I make and craft a lot, so am interested in manuscripts from an object perspective - I like to think about the bindings, how the paper is made, and how to repair damages. The course covered this a little, in discussing different medieval book types such as scrolls and 'pocket binding' (my favourite!), as well as printed books (which also interest me from a technological point of view). I would have liked more technical details, but it was a great introduction, and I now know what terminology to use to learn more.

The course focussed on the reader experience of using the books, and how the images and text worked together to create an engaging story. It was fun to learn more about some medieval Japanese tales, and I am now regretting not visiting the 'Tale of Genji' museum when I was in Uji a few years ago - though I doubt I would have got as much out of the visit back then. This gives me a good excuse to go back (and buy more tea while I'm there!).

The course had three modules, highlighting a different set of objects in each, and it was lovely to get such a close look at them. I did the free version of the course, but still got information about the assignments, so was very excited by the final project - to make your own illustrated manuscript using one of the binding techniques discussed during the course!

As I did the course for fun, I did not use the assignment prompt as intended. Instead of an artistic work, considering the interplay of text and image, I thought about how I could use the techniques in a workshop with young people.

I enjoyed the pocket and pouch style bindings, which fold pieces of paper in half so that you write only on one side (so the ink does not bleed through), but bind on the 'open' side of the fold, leaving a pocket between each page.

As a child, when learning to write, we put a lined piece of paper behind a plain sheet, as a guide to keep the writing straight on the page - I thought the pockets could be a good use for this. Once I have finished my prototype, I plan to print myself a grid guide to use to practice my Kanji - I can pretend that I don't need the boxes to make neat characters, no-one will ever know!

I very much enjoyed the course, and would recommend it. I added lots to my reading list, but am purposefully **not** learning more just yet, so I can concentrate on the modules in my degree!
